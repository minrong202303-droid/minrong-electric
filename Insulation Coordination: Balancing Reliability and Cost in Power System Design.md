Power system design is always a balance between reliability, safety, technical performance, and cost.

Insulation coordination is a good example of this balance. The objective is not simply to build the strongest possible insulation system, but to select insulation levels that are appropriate for the expected electrical stresses and the protection available.

What Creates Electrical Stress?

Power systems experience several types of voltage conditions.

Normal operating voltage is present continuously, while temporary overvoltages may occur following certain system events. Lightning and switching operations can create much faster transient overvoltages.

Each type of voltage stress can affect insulation differently.

This is why insulation coordination considers more than the nominal voltage of the network.

Protection and Insulation Work Together

A useful way to understand insulation coordination is to think of the protection system and equipment insulation as working together.

Surge arresters can limit certain overvoltages, while the insulation system provides the necessary withstand capability.

If equipment insulation is unnecessarily high, project costs may increase. If it is insufficient for the actual electrical environment, the risk of failure increases.

The engineering objective is to find an appropriate balance.

Why System Studies Matter

Modern insulation coordination studies can involve detailed analysis of the network and expected overvoltage conditions.

The 2023 edition of IEC 60071-2 includes updated guidance on detailed simulation, reflecting the increasing use of system studies in insulation coordination.

This can be particularly relevant for complex substations, long cable connections, transmission systems, and installations where switching or lightning conditions require closer evaluation.

Environmental Conditions Also Matter

Electrical insulation does not operate in a laboratory environment.

Altitude, pollution, humidity, temperature, and installation configuration can influence insulation performance. IEC 60071-2 includes application guidance addressing factors such as environmental conditions and altitude.

For outdoor high-voltage equipment, these factors can be especially important.

Building a Coordinated Design

A practical insulation coordination process should consider:

The highest system voltage
Expected operating and transient overvoltages
Equipment withstand characteristics
Surge arrester protection levels
Grounding and installation configuration
Environmental conditions
Applicable technical standards

The result should be a coordinated relationship between the electrical system and the equipment connected to it.

Conclusion

Insulation coordination may sound like a highly specialized topic, but the underlying idea is straightforward: electrical equipment should have insulation suitable for the voltage stresses it may encounter, while protective devices should help keep those stresses within acceptable limits.

Getting that balance right is an important part of designing reliable and cost-effective power systems.
